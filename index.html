Tariffs and the Iran War Have Cost the Average American Household $2,770 So Far

Since the U.S. put in place significantly higher tariffs in 2025 and the Iran war started in March 2026, the average cost per U.S. household is $2,770 more for fuel and tariffed goods, measured through October 25, 2026.

The bigger tax refunds from the One Big Beautiful Bill (OBBBA) in early 2026 give back about $375 of that per household (IRS: $49.8 billion more in refunds through May 8, 2026, than a year earlier, divided by 132.7 million households).

We are generally using the total dollar figure divided by 132.7 million households (Census), a similar method as the Brown University researchers and Moody’s. Updated daily as new data come in.

Iran war energy costs

How much more did the U.S. pay for fuel than it would have without the war? For what fuel would have cost, I use the Energy Information Administration’s own forecast from February 5, 2026, a few weeks before the war. EIA expected gas to peak around $3.06 this summer and fall to $2.84 by October. Instead, it was $4.28 on October 19.

For each day since March 1, I take the actual price minus EIA’s pre-war forecast, and multiply by the gallons the United States actually used. Prices come from EIA’s weekly retail surveys for gasoline and diesel. EIA doesn’t publish a retail jet fuel price, so I use its daily Gulf Coast spot price. Gallons come from EIA’s weekly consumption data.

We are using a relatively crude method by assuming 100% passthrough (e.g. assuming business fuel costs ultimately reach U.S. households). Some costs may instead be absorbed in business margins or passed through after the period measured. The figure measures the scale of the fuel-cost increase, rather than verified household spending. Brown’s Iran war tracker and Moody’s count fuel costs the same way.

Tariffs

For tariffs, our calculation relies on a new report from the New York Federal Reserve from October 6. We use NY Fed researchers’ estimated tariff effects on goods price levels and multiply those effects by BEA consumer spending data (PCE) over the period since early 2025. The authors find tariffs raised prices on 67 categories of consumer goods by 2.9% as of February 2026. After the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs, the NY Fed projected that the increase in goods prices relative to the no-Trump-tariffs counterfactual fell to about 2.1% in August and sits around 2.2% in October.

I apply that price increase to what Americans spend each month on those goods, and add it up from February 2025 to today. The 67 categories cover 75% of consumer goods spending (not counting gas or restaurant meals). I only count the impact for that 75% of goods NY Fed analyzed, and zero out the other 25%. Because the spending we observe already includes the tariff increase, I convert the NY Fed’s price effect (p, a percent change in the price level) into the share of spending that is tariff cost using p/(1+p) instead of p. That results in a cost of about $1,200 per household ($664 measured through February 2026 and $537 from the NY Fed’s projected path since then). This is the estimated extra cost of buying the goods households actually bought, including the NY Fed’s projected effects after February 2026. The 75% coverage share comes from 2022 CPI weights, so applying it to today’s spending is a rough estimate. BEA spending data run through August 2026, so later months use that month’s spending. While the underlying model comes from the New York Fed’s published report, these calculations are the authors’ own and are not associated with the New York Federal Reserve Bank.

This is also on the low end of estimates! The conservative leaning Tax Foundation puts the total 2025 and 2026 cost for the average household at around $1,800 total (Tax Foundation: $1,000 in 2025 and $820 in 2026).

What’s not included

  • Military costs. Moody’s puts military costs at $360 per household, e.g., total cost of the campaign so far divided by number of total households. But we are excluding these costs because they don’t show up in family budgets this year.
  • Food. The war disrupted fertilizer shipments from the Gulf, and USDA has raised its 2026 grocery inflation forecast.
  • Higher interest rates. These mostly hit people who buy or refinance a home. Mortgage rates are 7.23%, up from 6.05% in February. On a $330,000 loan, that’s about $3,090 more a year. Moody’s estimates higher rates have cost the economy about $540 per household.
